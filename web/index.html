The Hypsometric Principle: Why "1 Foot" Does Not Equal Constant Water

Because desert canyon reservoirs have V-shaped and stepped amphitheater bathymetry, the marginal water volume stored per foot of elevation rise ($\frac{dV}{dz} = A(z)$) increases by up to 600% from Dead Pool to Full Pool.

1. The "False Spring Rebound" Illusion

At low drought levels, a seasonal pulse of 600,000 AF shoots water elevation up by ~9 to 10 feet, creating false headlines of rapid recovery. Near full pool, that identical inflow raises the lake by barely 3.7 feet.

2. The Accelerating Drawdown Trap

When downstream consumption exceeds upstream inflow, the vertical drop accelerates exponentially. To satisfy fixed statutory deliveries (e.g. 7.5 MAF/yr compact delivery), dam gates must drop pool levels at increasingly rapid vertical speeds.

How a century of imaginary water allocations, emergency reservoir bailouts, and climate reality led to the post-2026 reckoning

1. 1922–1944: ALLOCATING "PAPER WATER"

The statutory origins of over-allocation and the 17.5 MAF baseline illusion

Historic Foundation

The legal architecture governing the basin began with the 1922 Colorado River Compact, signed in Santa Fe, New Mexico by delegates from the seven basin states and Herbert Hoover. It split the watershed at Lees Ferry, Arizona, apportioning 7.5 million acre-feet (MAF) annually to the Upper Basin (CO, WY, UT, NM) and 7.5 MAF to the Lower Basin (AZ, CA, NV) in perpetuity. Article III(b) authorized the Lower Basin to expand beneficial use by an additional 1.0 MAF/yr. In 1944, the Mexican Water Treaty added a sovereign guarantee of 1.5 MAF/yr delivered at Morelos Dam, stipulating that any deficiency be borne equally by Upper and Lower Basins (0.75 MAF each).

The Original Sin: Ignored Warnings & Missing Evaporation

Negotiators in Santa Fe anchored these legal entitlements to river gauge readings from the unusually wet period of 1905–1922, presuming average annual runoff exceeded 17.5 MAF/yr. USGS hydrologist E.C. LaRue published exhaustive field measurements warning that long-term flow was significantly lower, but commissioners dismissed his testimony to achieve political unanimity. Modern dendrochronology (tree-ring) studies and continuous USGS data verify that natural runoff averages only ~13.2 MAF/yr—and in the 21st century megadrought has averaged barely ~12.5 MAF/yr. Furthermore, the treaties omitted ~1.5 to 1.8 MAF/yr of reservoir evaporation and transit losses, locking the basin into 17.5+ MAF of statutory paper promises against ~12.5 MAF of physical water.

2. THE SENIORITY PECKING ORDER: WHO GETS CUT FIRST?

Prior appropriation, historical priority dates, and statutory subordination

Legal Hierarchy

Western water administration operates strictly on Prior Appropriation: "First in time, first in right." The user who first put river water to beneficial use holds senior legal priority. As the seven states developed throughout the 20th century, this doctrine produced an asymmetric hierarchy that dictates which states and users absorb curtailments when wet water fails:

  • California's Senior Fortress (1901 Rights): Southern California agricultural agencies—chief among them the Imperial Irrigation District (IID) and Coachella Valley Water District—hold "present perfected rights" established in 1901 that legally pre-date the 1922 Compact. Under federal statutory decree, IID alone holds 3.1 MAF of senior rights that are legally senior to virtually all municipal uses in Phoenix, Tucson, and Las Vegas.
  • Arizona's 1968 Junior Sacrifice: To secure federal authorization and funding to build the 336-mile Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal to Phoenix and Tucson, Arizona had to accept a harsh statutory compromise in the 1968 Colorado River Basin Project Act: CAP deliveries are legally junior to California's 4.4 MAF baseline allocation. When shortage triggers occur, Arizona must forfeit deliveries before California loses a single drop.
  • Upper Basin Hydrologic Snowpack Ceiling: Without massive downstream regulatory reservoirs directly above their diversions, Upper Basin irrigators and municipalities divert water in real time directly from mountain snowmelt tributaries. In dry summers, Colorado water commissioners enforce the priority system at local ditch headgates (e.g. Shoshone Power Plant calls curtail junior Denver and northern Front Range trans-mountain diversions). Consequently, the Upper Basin has never consumed its 7.5 MAF paper entitlement, remaining physically capped at ~4.0 to 4.5 MAF/yr.

3. 2000–2021: HIDING THE DEFICIT IN THE RESERVOIRS

How the structural deficit drained 37 million acre-feet of emergency storage

The 20-Year Drain

If legal allocations and systemic consumptive use exceeded natural inflows by millions of acre-feet every single year, why did the river system not collapse decades ago? Water managers drained the system's massive storage reservoirs to mask the annual deficit.

In 1999, Lake Powell and Lake Mead were 95% full, holding over 50 million acre-feet—representing nearly four full years of total river runoff stored in reserve. That massive multi-year savings account allowed Lower Basin cities and agricultural districts to continue consuming ~15.0 MAF annually while climate change, warming temperatures, and reduced snowpack suppressed river yield to ~12.5 MAF. The ~2.5 MAF annual structural deficit was silently withdrawn from reservoir storage year after year.

4. 2022–2024: DEAD POOL THREATS & EMERGENCY BUYOUTS

Federal crisis intervention, mandatory Tier cuts, and fallowing subsidies

Crisis Management

By mid-2022, the savings account was nearly exhausted. Powell and Mead plunged to 25% combined capacity. Lake Powell sank to within 32 feet of losing hydroelectric generation at Glen Canyon Dam (elevation 3,490 ft), and Bureau of Reclamation engineers warned of "dead pool" (elevation 3,370 ft)—the catastrophic threshold where water levels drop below dam outlet penstocks, halting downstream river flow entirely.

Mandatory Cuts Crush Arizona: The federal government declared unprecedented Tier 1 and Tier 2 shortages under the 2019 Drought Contingency Plan. Because of CAP's 1968 junior status, Arizona lost ~592,000 AF of water (>20% of its total Colorado River allocation), forcing central Arizona farmers to fallow tens of thousands of acres, while senior California sustained zero mandatory reductions.
The $1.2 Billion Federal Buyout: In June 2022, Reclamation Commissioner Camille Touton directed the seven basin states to slash consumption by 2.0 to 4.0 MAF within 60 days or face unilateral federal operational cuts. Rather than ignite an acrimonious Supreme Court fight over California's senior 1901 rights, the Biden administration committed $1.2 Billion from the Inflation Reduction Act, paying agricultural districts ~$400 per acre-foot to temporarily leave fields dry, contracting 3.0 MAF of conservation through 2026.

5. 2026 & BEYOND: THE END OF PRETEND WATER

Post-2026 operations, evaporation accounting, and permanent demand management

Post-2026 Cliff

The federal buyout subsidies, the 2007 Interim Guidelines, and the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans all expire simultaneously on December 31, 2026.

"You cannot drink paper water." Reservoirs can no longer be drawn down to bridge imaginary water rights, and federal taxpayers cannot subsidize fallowed agricultural land indefinitely.

In the contentious post-2026 operational negotiations, the 7 Basin States, 30 sovereign Native American Tribal Nations, and the Bureau of Reclamation are confronting the mathematical imperative: permanently cutting 2.0 to 4.0 million acre-feet of annual consumptive use. For the first time in century-old compact history, Lower Basin allocations will formally deduct ~1.5 MAF/yr of reservoir evaporation. Municipalities from Denver and Salt Lake City to Phoenix and Los Angeles are investing tens of billions in industrial-scale direct potable reuse, agricultural ditch piping, turf removal ordinances, and desalinization to realign civilization with the water volume nature actually delivers.

3. NOTEBOOK HOSTING ARCHITECTURE & OPEN-SCIENCE DISTRIBUTION

How research notebooks are rendered, distributed, and executed across web and cloud environments

Option A: Static HTML Web Hosting (Current Implementation)

The portal pre-renders every Jupyter notebook into standalone, zero-dependency HTML files inside web/notebooks/ using Pygments and KaTeX. This requires zero Python or Jupyter installation for portal visitors, loads instantly on mobile/desktop, and can be hosted statically on GitHub Pages, Netlify, or any web server.

Option B: Google Colab (Zero-Setup Interactive Execution)

Researchers and students can open any .ipynb notebook directly in Google Colab with 1 click. Colab runs code in a free Google Cloud sandbox, allowing users to rerun live USGS API queries, retrain K-Means clusters, or test new lag parameters without configuring a local Python environment.

Option C: Project Jupyter nbviewer (Official Jupyter Service)

Project Jupyter maintains nbviewer.org, an official, free cloud service. By entering the public GitHub repository URL, nbviewer dynamically compiles notebooks on demand with mathematical KaTeX formulas and high-resolution figures.

Option D: MyBinder (Interactive Cloud JupyterLab)

mybinder.org builds reproducible Docker containers directly from a GitHub repository's environment.yml or requirements.txt, launching a full interactive JupyterLab environment in the user's browser.
