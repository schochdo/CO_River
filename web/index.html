⚖️ The Law of the River & Legal Framework

The treaties, interstate compacts, statutes, and court decrees governing the Colorado River

1. The 1922 Colorado River Compact

Foundation

Negotiated in Santa Fe, New Mexico and signed by representatives of the seven basin states and Herbert Hoover, the Compact fundamentally divided the river at Lees Ferry, Arizona into the Upper Basin (Colorado, Wyoming, Utah, New Mexico) and Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada).

2. The "Paper Water" Structural Deficit

Core Dilemma

The Compact's fatal flaw was its reliance on gauge records from the anomalously wet period of 1905–1922. Modern tree-ring reconstructions and 21st-century USGS hydrologic records demonstrate that the long-term natural flow of the Colorado River is only ~12.3 to 13.5 million acre-feet. Allocating 15.0 MAF to the states plus 1.5 MAF to Mexico created an institutional structural deficit of over 2.0 MAF annually, steadily depleting Lake Mead and Lake Powell.

3. The 1944 Mexican Water Treaty

International

Guaranteed Mexico a minimum annual delivery of 1.5 million acre-feet of Colorado River water across the international boundary, delivered primarily at Morelos Dam near Yuma, Arizona. Under Minutes 319 and 323, the U.S. and Mexico established shared shortage guidelines and binational environmental restoration flows for the Colorado River Delta.

4. Prior Appropriation & Senior Rights (The Shoshone Call)

Western Doctrine

Western water law operates strictly on "first in time, first in right." Located in Glenwood Canyon, the Shoshone Hydroelectric Power Plant holds a 1902 senior water right for 1,250 cfs. Although non-consumptive (the water passes through the turbines back into the river), when Shoshone places a "call" on the river, junior trans-mountain diverters upstream (including Denver Water's Moffat and Roberts Tunnels) are legally forced to halt or curtail their diversions to let wet water stay in the riverbed.

5. Post-2026 Operations Negotiations

Current Horizon

The 2007 Interim Guidelines and the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans (DCP) governing operations at Lake Powell and Lake Mead expire on December 31, 2026. The seven basin states (divided between the Upper Division and Lower Division alternative proposals) along with 30 sovereign Native American Tribal Nations and the federal government are currently negotiating permanent, binding annual reductions of 2 to 4 million acre-feet to prevent the system from hitting dead pool.
